## Your Score Is Only Half The Evaluation

The feedback you write is assessed for accuracy, clarity, and usefulness to the annotator. A correct score paired with vague or generic feedback is still a weak review.

Before submitting, ask: could the annotator read this and know exactly what to do differently next time? If not, make it more specific and actionable.

## Be Careful With 1s, 2s, And 5s

Scores at the ends of the scale get the most scrutiny. A 1 or 2 says the work failed. A 5 says it is exemplary. Both need to be clearly earned.

- Score against the rubric tier by tier. Do not score on overall impression.
- Only submit a 1, 2, or 5 when the rubric language supports it. Hesitation is a signal to slow down.
- If still unsure after re-reading the rubric, ask in the corresponding Slack channel, or skip the task and let it cycle to someone else.
- Never guess a score to clear the queue. A wrong score at either extreme is harder to undo than a skipped task.

Full reviewer guidelines, including scoring templates, are not published yet. Until they are, grade against the rubric on the task page.

## What Good Feedback Looks Like

Do this:

- **Actionable** — say what the annotator should have done, not just what was wrong.
- **Specific** — point at the exact moment, segment, mark, or phrase you mean.
- **Comparative** — describe what good would have looked like on this task.
- **Friendly** — write to a colleague, not at them. Name what worked before what did not.
- **Grounded in the rubric** — tie the note to the tier language that drove your score.

Avoid this:

- One-word verdicts: "wrong," "fine," "bad pair."
- Restating the score without explaining it.
- Global judgments of the annotator rather than the work.
- Copy-pasted boilerplate that could apply to any task.
- Listing a problem with no path to fixing it.

## The Three-Part Pattern

1. **What worked** — one honest sentence about what the annotator got right.
2. **What missed and where** — name the exact spot and why it falls short of the rubric tier.
3. **What good would look like** — describe the corrected version concretely, so they can compare their work against it.

## Worked Example

Weak: "Alignment is off, score 2."

Strong: "Nice call keeping this pair, the connection is real. The alignment is about a second early: the shared moment lands at 0:04 in the response but 0:05 in the original, so they don't hit together on playback. Dragging the response window one second later would have put both on the same beat and moved this into the 4 range."

A second pattern, on a rejected pair: "The subject and setting match, so this was a reasonable pair to attempt. The issue is orientation: the behind-the-scenes clip is horizontal and the final shot is vertical, so they cannot sit side by side without letterboxing. A valid pair would use two clips shot in the same orientation of the same moment."
