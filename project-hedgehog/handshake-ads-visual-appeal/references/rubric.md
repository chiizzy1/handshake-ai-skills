## Core Concept

This question measures **pull**: how strongly each picture makes you stop mid-scroll and look.

It is subjective on purpose. Taste is expected and there is no single right answer. The requirement is
not that you reach a particular verdict. The requirement is that you look honestly and can **name your
reason** from the pull list below.

The question is one of several rating tasks over the same ad images. The others own defects, realism,
ad effectiveness, and the overlay. Staying inside your own lane is most of the skill here.

## Task Shape

- Two finished ad images side by side: **Output A** and **Output B**. Click either to magnify.
- Question: "Which image's content is more striking and engaging to look at? Ignore any overlaid text or graphics."
- Five buttons:
  1. Output A — clearly more appealing
  2. Output A — slightly more appealing
  3. Similarly good/bad
  4. Output B — slightly more appealing
  5. Output B — clearly more appealing
- One required feedback field: "In a sentence or two, say what made the winner more appealing — or why they're equally strong."

There is no prompt, no reference image, and no instruction to check. Nothing here is about compliance.

## The Routine

About half a minute per item.

1. **First impression at full size.** Which one would stop your scroll? On this question your first
   impression is the main instrument. Do not treat it as a bias to suppress. Most calls get made here.
2. **Guard scan, 10 to 15 seconds.** Two checks before you commit:
   - Is the pull coming from something out of scope, such as a slick headline or badge in the overlay,
     or from liking the product itself?
   - Glance across both pictures for appeal-killers you may have missed: muddy light, clutter, dead
     staging, an artificial look, or a defect in the focal area.
3. **Answer using the anchors.** If the scan found nothing, go with your first impression.
4. **Write one or two specific sentences** naming what created or killed the pull.

The order matters. The guard scan exists to test a first impression you already formed. If you start by
hunting for flaws, you have turned this into the defects task and your answer will be wrong for the
right-sounding reasons.

## What Creates Pull

Judge these. Every winner you pick has to be explainable by at least one of them.

| Source | What to look at |
|---|---|
| Focal point | Is there one place the eye lands first? Was it framed on purpose or did it just end up there? |
| Composition and hierarchy | Does the arrangement lead the eye through the picture, or scatter it? Is there balance? |
| Light and exposure | Sharp focus and correct exposure where it matters. Does the light give the subject shape, or flatten it? |
| Color | Does the palette work together and support the subject? |
| Posing and proportions | Do people and objects sit convincingly in the scene? |
| Cohesion | Do the elements read as one picture, or as parts assembled together? |

## Appeal-Killers

What the guard scan looks for:

- Muddy or flat light. Washed-out or fighting colors.
- Clutter. Competing elements with nowhere for the eye to land.
- Dead, static staging where the subject deserved drama.
- An artificial, implausible look that breaks the scene.
- A defect in the focal area that is visible at normal size.

The last one is the only place a rendering defect enters this question at all, and it enters as a
**look** problem, not as a flaw tally.

## The Five Buttons

Pick the row that matches what you found.

| What you found | Your answer |
|---|---|
| You would stop scrolling for one and pass the other without a glance | Clearly more appealing |
| Several advantages line up on one side (light, color, and composition all better) | Clearly more appealing |
| Both hold the eye, and one has a single aesthetic edge you can name | Slightly more appealing |
| Both are weak, but one is noticeably less flat | Slightly more appealing |
| Equally striking or equally flat, with no edge you can name | Similarly good/bad |

If you pick a side, your feedback must name the edge. If you cannot name one, your answer is
*Similarly good/bad*. Do not force a winner between two equally flat images, and do not call a tie on a
real edge because the pair felt close.

Two weak images are not automatically a tie. Both weak with one noticeably less flat is a slight win.
The tie is for *equally* flat, not for *both* flat.

### Zoom Discipline

Zoom in to check details, not to find an edge. These pairs often differ in file resolution, so one side
may look sharper at maximum zoom. That is a file property, not an appeal edge. Use zoom to identify what
a suspected defect or detail is, then judge it at normal viewing size. **If you need 200 percent zoom to
justify a preference, the answer is Similarly good/bad.**

The guideline's own pairs bear this out. In both of its tie cases one file was substantially larger
than the other (1600px against 1024px, and 2096px against 1360px), and the correct answer was still a
tie. A resolution advantage never earns a win by itself.

## Worked Example

*Output A is a flawless, perfectly exposed product photo, but it is static and flat and nothing leads
your eye. Output B stops your scroll with warm low light and a strong diagonal composition, though a
faint smear artifact sits in one corner.*

**Answer: Output B, slightly or clearly depending on how hard it pulls.**

Being defect-free does not make A engaging. B's faint corner smear does not hurt the look at normal
viewing size, so it does not cancel the light and depth that make B arresting. Name those in the
feedback. If the artifact sat on the focal point instead, it would count against B.

This one example carries most of the task. A clean, boring picture loses to an arresting one with a
harmless flaw, and the location of a defect decides whether it matters at all.

## Out Of Scope

Five things belong to other rating tasks and must not drive your answer or appear in your feedback.

**1. Flaw count.** A different task covers defects. A rendering defect matters here only when it visibly
hurts the look at normal viewing size, such as a distracting artifact sitting in the focal area. A
slightly flawed image can still be the more arresting picture, and often is.

**2. Realism.** "Looks AI-generated" is a different task. A clean, convincingly photographic image can
still be flat and dull. A stylized or non-photoreal image can win on appeal when both outputs share that
medium. A style choice such as black-and-white, a color tint, or an illustration look is not a defect.

**3. Ad effectiveness.** Whether the image would work as an advertisement, or sell the product, is
another task's question. A gorgeous picture that barely features the product can still win here.

**4. The overlay.** Text and graphics laid over the picture are out of scope, including how good they
look. Judge the picture behind them. A misspelled headline over a stunning photo does not lower your
answer, because the spelling of overlaid text is not part of this question at all.

**5. Your own taste.** In the product, the category, the color temperature, or the whole style. You may
find all of it tacky. Judge how much pull this execution has, not whether you would buy it or hang it on
your wall.

No style is inherently more appealing than another. Loud maximalism executed with control and energy
beats a quiet minimal shot with dead, empty staging, even when you personally dislike maximalism. The
comparison is always execution against execution. And style differences never cancel out into
*Similarly good/bad* by themselves. Two different styles, one executed better, is a win for the better
execution.

And one more, from the cheat sheet: **do not pixel-peep.** Sharpness you can only see at maximum zoom is
a file property, not an edge. Magnify to confirm whether a defect sits in the focal area and hurts the
look at normal size. Do not magnify to find a winner.

## Special Cases

**Text-only pairs.** When both images are essentially just words on a background, judge the canvas
behind the words: its color, texture, and energy. The words are still out of scope.

**Mixed strength.** One image can have the better light while the other has the better composition. That
is a real *similarly good/bad*, or a slight win to whichever advantage actually stopped you first. Say
which one it was.

**Both flat.** Two boring pictures are only *similarly good/bad* when they are equally flat. If one is
noticeably less flat than the other, that is a slight win and you name what makes it less flat. Say
plainly when neither one holds the eye.

**Both excellent.** Same logic in the other direction. If both stop you and you cannot name an edge, they
are similarly good.

## Rejections

Only three reject reasons exist:

- **Image did not load.** An image fails to load and the panel shows a load error.
- **Inappropriate content.** Nudity or sexually explicit poses, hate symbols or hate speech, or graphic
  violence.
- **Outputs are different mediums (photorealistic vs illustration).** One output is photographic and the
  other is a flat illustration or graphic. Those two cannot be fairly compared on appeal. Two
  illustrations are the same medium, so compare them normally. Two photographs are the same medium. A
  black-and-white or tinted photo is still a photo.

Never reject for an ugly, flawed, or boring image. That is what your vote records. Never reject for
people in swimwear or underwear, for content that is merely suggestive, or because the decision is close
or difficult.

## Feedback Rules

Name the pull, say where it is in the picture, and say why it decided you. One or two sentences.

The form has a minimum length, but what reviewers check is specificity. **Vague feedback on a correct
answer still fails review.** For ties, describe what both images do well or both do badly. A tie note
has to be as specific as a win note.

- Good: "B's low golden light and the diagonal of the road give it depth. A is evenly lit and flat."
- Good tie note: "Both are crisp studio shots with balanced palettes and clear focal points, so they are equally strong."
- Bad: "B looks nicer." This names nothing.
- Bad: "A, because I love this shade of blue." Your palette taste is not an edge.

Style requirements:

- Write like an average person with an eye for pictures, not an art critic.
- Point at a place in the frame. "The light on the bottle," "the road running through it," "the top left corner."
- No rubric vocabulary: focal hierarchy, compositional balance, visual pull, cohesion, appeal-killers.
- No out-of-scope reasons: more real, fewer artifacts, better product visibility, readable text.
- No em dashes and no semicolons. Use periods.
- No AI power words: delve, tapestry, meticulous, striking visual narrative.
- No absolutes: "perfect lighting" becomes "the light is much better."
- Prefer "while" to "whereas."

## Good vs Bad Calls

Four worked cases from the guideline. The feedback lines quoted as correct are the guideline's own
exemplars. Image URLs are in
`HANDSHAKE-AI/vs-1786690831-260813-ads-creative-i2i-q4-visual-appeal.md` if you want to look at the
pairs.

### Case 1: Same subject, different staging

*Three identical wellness sticks with flowers on marble. A stages one hero tube up front with the other
two softly defocused behind it under a visible light ray. B lines all three up flat and equally sharp.*

**Correct: Output A — clearly more appealing.**

> "A stages one hero tube up front with the other two softly defocused behind it, under a visible light
> ray, so the eye lands in one place. B lines all three up flat and equally sharp, so nothing leads."

Both show the same objects. The difference is staging. A builds depth, atmosphere, and hierarchy while
B is a catalog row under flat light. Three advantages on one side makes it *clearly*, not *slightly*.

Observed in the actual images: B also carries an overlaid headline ("Relief in Your Pocket") and its
product labels are soft and partly garbled. **Neither appears in the correct feedback.** The call was
made on staging, and the out-of-scope material was left out of the note entirely. That is the model to
copy. Do not mention an overlay or a text defect even when it is the first thing you notice.

**The wrong call: Similarly good/bad, "Same tubes, same flowers, same marble. There is no real
difference."** That annotator listed the objects and stopped. Appeal is about how the picture presents
the subject, not which objects are in it. **Two images of the same subject can sit a full anchor apart on
staging, light, and hierarchy alone.**

### Case 2: Judge the picture behind the graphics

*A benchtop machine tool. A is a workshop scene carrying overlaid feature callouts. B is a dark studio
shot.*

**Correct: Output B — clearly more appealing.**

> "B's single directional light pulls crisp highlights off the metal against the deep teal backdrop, and
> the machine owns the frame. A's bench is cluttered with props under flat light, and nothing is staged."

Ignore A's callout bubbles however informative they are. Behind them the picture is a busy workbench
where the blueprint, tools, and props all compete for the eye.

Observed in the actual images: A is a fully built ad, with a brand logo, a two-line headline, three
icon callouts, two labels, and a red call-to-action button. Stripping all of that leaves a warm but
flat bench shot. B places its props deliberately (a caliper, a gear, a ring set below the machine) and
they read as staging rather than clutter. Same kind of objects, opposite effect, decided by
arrangement and light.

**The wrong call: Output A — slightly, "A looks more professional. The labeled feature callouts make it
feel like a finished ad."** Two scope errors at once. The callouts are overlaid graphics, and "feels like
a finished ad" is the ad-effectiveness question. Strip both and A is the cluttered, flatter picture.

### Case 3: Equal craft, different palette

*The same stack of coffee boxes in two splash shots. A is icy and cool-toned, B is warm with a coffee
wave.*

**Correct: Similarly good/bad.**

> "Both freeze a dramatic splash around the same stack with equal control: crisp droplets, strong
> contrast, one clear focal point each. One runs cold and one runs warm, but neither is better made."

Both are excellent, and when both sides are equally strong the answer is a tie. The only nameable
difference is cold palette versus warm palette, which is a style choice rather than an execution gap.

Observed in the actual images: A freezes a water splash with ice cubes and beans against near-black,
B freezes a coffee splash with steam over a bed of beans, and B additionally carries an overlaid
wordmark bar. Both have equally soft package text. Everything that differs is either style or out of
scope, which is exactly why it ties.

**The wrong call: Output B — clearly, "Coffee should feel warm. The icy look is wrong for this
product."** That is a judgment about what suits the product, which is ad effectiveness, stacked on a
palette preference. **When the only difference you can name is color mood, the answer is Similarly
good/bad.**

### Case 4: Two flat images are also a tie

*Two near-identical talking-head frames from the same creative. A has a patterned teal backdrop, B has a
plain gradient and a tighter crop.*

**Correct: Similarly good/bad.**

> "Both are flat, evenly lit webcam-style frames of the same speaker, with no staging, no mood, and no
> focal idea in either. A's patterned backdrop is decoration and does not add pull."

The middle button covers pairs that are equally unappealing. Saying that both are flat is a complete
answer.

Observed in the actual images: A is the larger file by a wide margin (1184x2096 against B's 768x1360)
and has a decorative border, textured backdrop, and scattered graphic shapes. It still ties. More
pixels and more ornament are not more pull.

**The wrong call: Output A — slightly, "A's patterned background adds some visual interest."** That
forced an edge rather than use the tie button. **Decoration behind the same flat subject is not an
aesthetic edge, because the eye still lands on the same evenly lit speaker either way.**

Read Case 4 against the anchor row "both are weak, but one is noticeably less flat." Less flat means the
picture itself does more: light, staging, depth, a focal idea. A busier background is not less flat.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Rating the defects task by accident.** The most common error. You find a warped hand, and the image
   with the warped hand loses. Ask whether the defect actually hurt the look at normal size. If it did
   not, it is not your reason.
2. **Rating realism by accident.** "A looks like a real photograph and B looks AI." That is a different
   question. The AI-looking one can be the more arresting picture.
3. **Rating the ad by accident.** "You can see the product better in A." Not this question.
4. **Judging the overlay.** Reading the headline, noticing its font, or docking an image for a typo.
5. **Inflating severity.** One nameable edge is a slight win. Reserve *clearly* for a gap you saw
   instantly or for several advantages stacking on one side.
6. **Refusing to tie.** *Similarly good/bad* is a real answer here, for both-strong and both-weak pairs.
7. **Tying to avoid the work.** The opposite error. If you can name an edge, pick the side.
8. **Pixel-peeping to break a tie.** Zoom-only sharpness is not an edge.
9. **Analyzing before looking.** Skipping the first impression and going straight to a checklist. The
   first impression is the measurement. Everything after it is a check on that measurement.
10. **Feedback with no location.** "The composition is better" does not say where. "Your eye lands on
    the burger and stays there" does.
11. **Tying two weak images out of habit.** Both flat is only a tie when they are *equally* flat. One
    noticeably less flat than the other is a slight win.
12. **Overriding a good first impression.** The guard scan is a check, not a veto. If it finds nothing,
    the first impression stands.
13. **Vague feedback on a right answer.** The vote can be correct and the item still fails review
    because the note named nothing.
14. **Listing objects instead of judging the picture.** "Same tubes, same flowers, same marble, no real
    difference" is an inventory, not a read. Same subject, different staging, is a real gap.
15. **Counting decoration as pull.** A busier or patterned background behind the same flat subject does
    not move the eye anywhere new.

## Decision Checklist

- [ ] Both images loaded, content is allowed, and both share a medium. No reject needed.
- [ ] First impression formed at full size and trusted as the main instrument.
- [ ] The winner's edge is named from the pull list.
- [ ] Guard scan of 10 to 15 seconds run: out-of-scope pull check, then missed appeal-killer check.
- [ ] Reason survives the scope strip: not defect count, realism, ad effectiveness, overlay, or taste.
- [ ] Not decided by zoom-only sharpness. Nothing required 200 percent zoom to justify.
- [ ] Severity matches the anchors. Several advantages is clearly. One is slightly.
- [ ] Feedback names the pull and where it sits in the frame, in one or two plain sentences. Tie notes are specific too.
- [ ] Answer given in the chat. No workspace file modified.
